How the calculator works it out

The calculator follows the same order as the tax law, and the working table under the result shows each line, so you can check it against your own payslip or Form 16.

  1. Start from gross income for the year: salary before any deduction, or other income.
  2. Subtract the standard deduction if the income is salary: ₹75,000 in the new regime, ₹50,000 in the old one.
  3. Old regime only, subtract Section 80C and 80D, each capped: up to ₹1,50,000 for 80C investments and up to ₹25,000 for 80D health insurance. What is left is taxable income.
  4. Apply the slab rates in the tables below, each rate only to the part of taxable income that falls inside its band.
  5. Take off the Section 87A rebate if taxable income is at or under the threshold (₹12 lakh new, ₹5 lakh old).
  6. Add 4% health and education cess on the tax that remains. That total is your tax for the year; gross income minus it is the take-home figure.

The old regime allows Sections 80C and 80D (modelled here) plus others this calculator doesn't cover, such as HRA and home loan interest, and its standard deduction is ₹50,000 for salaried income. Its Section 87A rebate cancels tax only up to ₹5,00,000 taxable income.

The new-regime slab tax is built up band by band: nothing on the first ₹4,00,000, ₹20,000 on the next ₹4,00,000 at 5%, ₹40,000 on the next ₹4,00,000 at 10%, and ₹33,750 on the last ₹2,25,000 at 15%, which makes ₹93,750. In the old regime the same person pays ₹12,500 on ₹2,50,000 to ₹5,00,000 at 5%, ₹1,00,000 on ₹5,00,000 to ₹10,00,000 at 20%, and ₹82,500 on the last ₹2,75,000 at 30%. Even with both deductions used in full, the new regime costs ₹1,05,300 less a year here.

How much in deductions the old regime needs

At a ₹15,00,000 salary the old regime only catches up when its taxable income falls to ₹9,06,250, where its slab tax equals the new regime's ₹93,750. That takes ₹5,43,750 of deductions on top of the ₹50,000 standard deduction. Sections 80C and 80D together allow at most ₹1,75,000, so the rest would have to come from deductions this calculator does not model, mainly HRA and home loan interest. If you do not have those, the new regime is almost certainly cheaper at this income. If you do, add them up before you choose, because the answer turns on that total rather than on your salary alone.

Just above the ₹12 lakh line

A salary of ₹13,00,000 in the new regime leaves ₹12,25,000 of taxable income after the standard deduction. That is above ₹12,00,000, so the 87A rebate no longer applies and the calculator shows the full slab tax of ₹63,750 plus ₹2,550 cess, ₹66,300 in all. The law softens this cliff with marginal relief, which this calculator does not apply, so for taxable income a little over ₹12 lakh your actual tax will be lower than the figure shown. Treat the result in this band as an upper limit and check it with the Income Tax Department's calculator.

When to use it

  • Choosing a regime at the start of the year. Salaried employees tell their employer which regime to use for tax deducted from salary; run your income through both tabs first.
  • Weighing a raise or a job offer. Enter the new gross figure to see how much of the increase you keep, and whether it crosses the ₹12 lakh rebate line.
  • Deciding whether an 80C investment is worth it for tax. In the old regime, compare the result with 80C at zero and at ₹1,50,000; in the new regime it makes no difference.

What this estimate leaves out

This is a slab-and-rebate estimate, not a filing. It does not include surcharge on incomes above ₹50 lakh, the exact marginal relief just above either rebate threshold, special rates on capital gains or similar income, or old-regime deductions beyond Sections 80C and 80D such as HRA, home loan interest or NPS. Use it to plan, and a CA or the Income Tax Department's own calculator for a return you're filing.

Two simplifications in the inputs: Section 80D is capped at ₹25,000, the limit for a policy covering yourself and your family when you are under 60, so the higher limits for senior citizens and for a separate policy for your parents are not included. And the age choice only appears in the old regime, because the new regime's slabs are the same at every age.

Frequently asked questions

Is income up to ₹12 lakh really tax-free in the new regime?

Yes, for FY 2026-27. The slab rates on taxable income up to ₹12,00,000 add up to ₹60,000 of tax, and the Section 87A rebate cancels exactly that amount, so the net tax is zero. For a salaried taxpayer, the ₹75,000 standard deduction pushes the effective tax-free gross income up to ₹12,75,000.

Did Budget 2026 change the income tax slabs?

No. Union Budget 2026-27 (presented 1 February 2026) made no change to the income tax slabs, standard deduction or Section 87A rebate for either regime, so the FY 2025-26 figures carry over unchanged to FY 2026-27.

Which regime should I choose?

The new regime is the default and usually works out better if you claim few deductions. The old regime can work out better if your Section 80C, 80D and other deductions (including HRA and home loan interest, not modelled in this calculator) are large relative to your income. Run both and compare.

What is the Section 87A rebate?

A rebate that cancels tax up to a cap, provided taxable income is under a threshold: up to ₹60,000 for taxable income up to ₹12,00,000 in the new regime, or up to ₹12,500 for taxable income up to ₹5,00,000 in the old regime. Just above either threshold, marginal relief (not modelled here) limits how fast the tax reappears.

Is the standard deduction the same in both regimes?

No. It's ₹75,000 in the new regime and ₹50,000 in the old regime, and either way it only applies to salaried income, not business or other income.
