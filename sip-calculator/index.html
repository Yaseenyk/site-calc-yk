How the projection is calculated

Future value = P x [((1 + i)^n - 1) / i] x (1 + i)

  • P is the amount invested each month.
  • i is the assumed monthly return: the annual return divided by 12, then by 100.
  • n is the number of months invested.

This is the standard formula for a series of equal monthly investments compounding at the assumed rate, with each instalment treated as invested at the start of its month (the usual SIP convention).

Worked example

₹5,000 a month for 10 years at an assumed 12% annual return: total invested is ₹6,00,000, projecting to about ₹11,61,695 — roughly ₹5,61,695 in projected returns, assuming the 12% holds for the full period.

What this leaves out

This is a projection based on one assumed, constant return rate. It does not account for expense ratios, exit load, taxes on capital gains, or the real-world variability of market returns from year to year, which rarely stays flat even when the long-run average matches your assumption.

Frequently asked questions

What is a SIP?

A Systematic Investment Plan is a fixed amount invested into a mutual fund at a set interval, usually monthly, rather than investing a lump sum at once. SIPs average your purchase price across market ups and downs over time.

How is SIP future value calculated?

FV = P x [((1 + i)^n - 1) / i] x (1 + i), where P is the monthly amount, i is the expected monthly return (annual return divided by 12 and by 100), and n is the number of months. The extra (1 + i) factor accounts for each instalment being invested at the start of its month, the usual SIP timing.

Is the return rate guaranteed?

No. The rate you enter is an assumption for projection purposes only. Mutual fund SIPs are market-linked, and the actual return can be higher or lower than any rate you assume here, including negative in a bad year.

Is SIP better than a lump sum investment?

Neither is universally better. A lump sum invested at the start can outperform a SIP in a rising market, since more money is invested earlier, but a SIP reduces the risk of investing everything right before a downturn. Many investors use both.

What return rate should I assume?

This is your own assumption to make, not something the calculator supplies. Equity mutual funds have historically returned in a wide range depending on the period measured; a conservative planning assumption is usually safer than an optimistic one.
