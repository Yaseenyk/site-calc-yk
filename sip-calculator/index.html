How a SIP projection is calculated

Future value = P x [((1 + i)^n - 1) / i] x (1 + i)

  • P is the amount invested each month.
  • i is the assumed monthly return: the annual return divided by 12, then by 100.
  • n is the number of months invested.

It adds up a series of equal monthly investments, each growing at the assumed rate from the start of its month until the end. The first instalment grows for all n months and the last for one.

How to use the SIP calculator

  1. Enter the monthly amount you could invest every month.
  2. Enter a return you assume. It is your assumption; no one can promise a return on a market-linked fund.
  3. Enter the years. The table shows the amount invested and the projected value at the end of each year.

Worked example: ₹5,000 a month for 10 years at an assumed 12% a year. You invest ₹6,00,000 (₹5,000 x 120 months). The projected value is ₹11,61,695, so ₹5,61,695 of it is projected growth, if the 12% held for the whole period.

SIP compared with a lump sum

Suppose you invest the same total, ₹6,00,000, either as ₹5,000 a month for 10 years or as one lump sum on day one, at an assumed 12% a year in both cases. The SIP projects to ₹11,61,695. The lump sum, compounded monthly, projects to ₹19,80,232. The lump sum is larger only because all of the money starts working on day one; most people do not have it on day one. A SIP fits the way income arrives. The compound interest calculator handles the lump sum.

What this leaves out

This is a projection from one assumed, constant return rate. Real market returns vary from year to year and can be negative, so the actual result will differ. It does not include fund expenses, exit loads, taxes on gains, or any rise in your monthly amount over time. Read the fund's documents before you invest.

Frequently asked questions

What is a SIP?

A Systematic Investment Plan is a fixed amount invested into a mutual fund at a set interval, usually monthly, instead of one lump sum. You buy in at many different prices over time rather than at one price.

How is SIP future value calculated?

FV = P x [((1 + i)^n - 1) / i] x (1 + i), where P is the monthly amount, i is the assumed monthly return (annual return divided by 12 and by 100), and n is the number of months. The extra (1 + i) factor treats each instalment as invested at the start of its month.

How much will a SIP of 5,000 a month be worth in 10 years?

It depends on the return you assume, which nobody can promise. At an assumed 12% a year, ₹5,000 a month for 10 years invests ₹6,00,000 and projects to ₹11,61,695. At an assumed 8% it projects to ₹9,20,828, and at 6% to ₹8,23,494.

Is the return rate guaranteed?

No. The rate you enter is an assumption for projection only. Mutual fund SIPs are market-linked, and the actual return can be higher or lower than any rate you assume here, including negative in a bad year.

What return rate should I assume?

That is your own assumption to make, and the calculator does not suggest one. Try a low, a middle and a high figure, as the table on this page does, and plan around the low one.

Does starting early matter?

Yes, because time does more of the work than the amount. At an assumed 12%, ₹5,000 a month for 30 years projects to ₹1,76,49,569, while 20 years projects to ₹49,95,740. The table on this page shows the cost of waiting.

Is SIP better than a lump sum investment?

Neither is better in every case. With the same return assumed, a lump sum invested on day one grows more than a SIP of the same total, because more money is invested earlier: ₹19,80,232 against ₹11,61,695 for ₹6,00,000 over 10 years at 12%. A SIP fits money that arrives monthly, and it avoids choosing one day to invest everything.

What is the difference between a SIP and a recurring deposit?

A recurring deposit pays a rate fixed by the bank. A mutual fund SIP buys fund units, whose value moves with the market, so there is no fixed rate. For a fixed-rate deposit, use the compound interest calculator.

Does this include taxes, fees and fund charges?

No. It shows the gross projection at the rate you enter. Fund expenses, exit loads and taxes on gains reduce what you keep, so read the fund's documents and a current tax guide.
